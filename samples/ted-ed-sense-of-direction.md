# Why do some people have a better sense of direction than others? - Oliver Baumann

![video_link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=I8XaYkRW1tA)

## 字幕

[00:06-00:12] In July of 2025, top athletes competed in a kilometers-long race—

[00:12-00:14] but it had no set path.

[00:14-00:32] That's because it was the World Orienteering Championships, and participants equipped with only maps and compasses were expected to find their way between far-off checkpoints at high speeds, weaving between trees, sprinting over rocks, and skirting cliffs.

[00:32-00:34] This might sound a little daunting.

[00:34-00:51] But whether you identify as a wayfinding whiz or get lost returning from the grocery store, your brain has a built-in navigation system that facilitates spatial skills, from sensing which direction is north to map reading and route learning.

[00:51-00:57] It consists of two major parts: the striatum and the hippocampus.

[00:57-01:02] The striatum is clued into sensory inputs, like nearby landmarks.

[01:02-01:12] With repetition, it comes to associate specific stimuli and actions with certain responses and outcomes, linking these cues with set routes.

[01:12-01:20] This forms navigation habits; for example, when you automatically take the same sequence of turns on your way home.

[01:20-01:27] The hippocampus, meanwhile, constructs continuous, dynamic mental maps of your environment.

[01:27-01:33] It helps you figure out how to get somewhere, even if you've never been or haven't taken that exact route before.

[01:33-01:39] Researchers realized the hippocampus’ importance in mental mapping in the 70s,

[01:39-01:49] while observing rats’ brain activity and noticing that specific neurons in the hippocampus fired when the rats were in certain spaces.

[01:49-02:01] The same thing also happened in complete darkness, indicating that these neurons, which came to be called “place cells,” formed cognitive maps independent of sensory input.

[02:01-02:11] It seems that activity in these areas during sleep represents a kind of place replay as the brain consolidates spatial information.

[02:11-02:17] And in 2005, researchers discovered additional types of navigational neurons

[02:17-02:20] in the region surrounding the hippocampus.

[02:20-02:30] This includes “grid cells,” which fire in a regular pattern as one moves, providing the brain with a kind of internal coordinate system.

[02:30-02:39] Together, the breakthrough discoveries that put place and grid cells on the map earned the 2014 Nobel Prize.

[02:39-02:51] But while the striatum and hippocampus form a navigation system that allows our brains to adapt between strategies depending on the task, we still struggle to find our ways.

[02:51-03:00] In the absence of strong external cues, people tend to make small errors that send them off course and into circles.

[03:00-03:04] And generally, some of us find ourselves lost more often than others.

[03:04-03:16] Most people can handle a striatum-centric, well-practiced route; but those who lean more on hippocampus-forward self-orienting tend to stand out as navigators.

[03:16-03:22] Twin studies suggest that genetics account for around 60% of the variation

[03:22-03:24] in our navigation abilities.

[03:24-03:32] These abilities appear independent of general intelligence, suggesting that navigation is its own distinct cognitive skill.

[03:32-03:38] And crucially, research indicates that it’s one that can be practiced and improved.

[03:38-03:51] Compared to people from suburban and rural areas, those who grew up in cities— especially ones with more uniform, grid-like layouts— appear to struggle more with navigating less ordered environments.

[03:51-04:00] And in a 2010 study, rugby players and martial artists were blindfolded and asked to navigate back to a starting point.

[04:00-04:13] The rugby players, who train at much larger scales, did so with more directional accuracy, suggesting that the difference in their spatial training improved their judgement across longer distances.

[04:13-04:19] London cab drivers, meanwhile, must learn what’s referred to as “the knowledge,”

[04:19-04:27] which consists of extensive routes and landmarks across the city’s infamously complex maze of streets.

[04:27-04:34] Those who successfully complete the training have been observed to experience significant hippocampal growth.

[04:34-04:39] But the brain’s malleability can be a double-edged sword.

[04:39-04:48] While GPS devices reliably get us around, they lead to worse spatial reconstructions than verbal directions and paper maps do.

[04:48-05:01] By allowing our brains to engage less actively with environmental cues, GPS may degrade the hippocampal navigation system and impair our own internal sense of direction.

[05:01-05:10] So, to help improve it, you can try putting your GPS away on more familiar routes and paying attention to distant landmarks.

[05:10-05:23] Instead of using turn-by-turn directions, consider experimenting with a paper map and exploring new places on foot, since physical movement helps the brain encode spatial information.

[05:23-05:27] Then, with a little practice, the next time you get turned around, maybe you can first look within yourself to find your way.
