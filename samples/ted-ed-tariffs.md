# What are tariffs, and how do they work?

![video_link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BSdEiOYvKpE)

## 字幕

[00:06-00:15] In 1930, as the Great Depression ravaged the United States, President Herbert Hoover made a bold economic decision.

[00:15-00:26] With the goal of supporting American farmers and manufacturers, he approved a sweeping 20% tax on all imported agricultural and industrial goods.

[00:26-00:36] He believed making foreign goods more expensive would encourage consumers to buy domestic, thus funneling cash into American businesses and creating more jobs.

[00:36-00:39] But Hoover's plan completely backfired.

[00:39-00:47] Other countries retaliated by taxing American goods, leading exports to fall and global trade to decline.

[00:47-00:54] In the end, this policy led American prices to spike and caused even more unemployment.

[00:54-01:03] These disastrous outcomes of the Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act were no surprise to the over 1,000 economists who publicly condemned the bill.

[01:03-01:09] But this wouldn’t be the last time a world leader was shocked by a tariff's consequences.

[01:09-01:12] So, what exactly is a tariff?

[01:12-01:17] A tariff is a tax an importer pays to bring foreign goods into a country.

[01:17-01:23] For example, if they import $1,000 worth of goods at a 10% tariff rate,

[01:23-01:29] they'll pay $100 to the government in addition to $1,000 to the seller.

[01:29-01:35] Foreign exporters could respond to a tariff by reducing their product’s price to keep an importer’s business.

[01:35-01:43] But if they don’t, importers bear this cost and then increase their prices so consumers will make up the difference.

[01:43-01:49] This may sound straightforward, but enacting a tariff kicks off a complicated economic chain reaction.

[01:49-01:59] So to keep things simple, let's look at some examples from just one country to explore some of the factors that make a tariff’s outcomes tricky to predict.

[01:59-02:04] First, tariffs can be applied to different countries at different rates.

[02:04-02:11] For example, in 2024, America had a 6% tariff on all wool entering the country.

[02:11-02:18] However, if that wool came from Mexico or South Korea, it would be tariff-free due to trade agreements.

[02:18-02:25] Meanwhile, importers getting wool from Russia could face a hefty 55.5% tariff.

[02:25-02:34] Policies like these might help domestic wool producers, or they might just encourage importers to buy from specific foreign producers.

[02:34-02:39] Things get even trickier when trying to predict how tariffs impact jobs.

[02:39-02:43] Sometimes the jobs they create come at a high cost.

[02:43-02:55] In 2018, the US imposed a tariff on washing machines that created 1,800 jobs and raised roughly $82 million in government revenue.

[02:55-03:01] However, it also increased the average price of washing machines by $86,

[03:01-03:05] costing consumers roughly $1.5 billion.

[03:05-03:13] This means consumers paid roughly $817,000 to create each of those jobs.

[03:13-03:18] In other cases, the jobs tariffs create come at the cost of existing jobs.

[03:18-03:25] In 2002, the Bush administration imposed tariffs as high as 30% on imported steel.

[03:25-03:36] Employment in US steel production had been decaying for decades, so by making foreign steel more expensive, the tariff sought to create new jobs for US steel manufacturers.

[03:36-03:39] Initially, the policy did slow down job loss.

[03:39-03:48] But since US steel still cost more than pre-tariff foreign steel, domestic industries that used this material had to increase their prices.

[03:48-03:55] This made their products less competitive internationally, where foreign manufacturers were still using cheap steel.

[03:55-04:05] Within a year of enacting the tariff, the US lost far more jobs in steel-related industries than they gained in steel-producing industries.

[04:05-04:11] Clearly, how other countries respond to tariffs is another big part of the ripple effect these policies have.

[04:11-04:19] One clear example comes from 2018, when the US attempted to address its trade deficit with China by taxing Chinese goods.

[04:19-04:25] While these tariffs protected domestic businesses and raised government revenue, they also set off a trade war.

[04:25-04:33] China retaliated by raising tariffs on US exporters, including farmers relying on Chinese markets for soybean sales.

[04:33-04:41] In the end, this led to higher prices for US consumers and a slight drop in America's GDP.

[04:41-04:42] Trade wars aren’t new.

[04:42-04:53] And even in the 18th and early 19th centuries, when certain tariffs did help struggling industries find their domestic footing, they were mostly used to raise government funds.

[04:53-04:59] But over the last 200 years, our global economy has become far more interdependent.

[04:59-05:08] Today, there are more manufacturing components being imported than ever before, many of which are used to make a wide variety of consumer goods.

[05:08-05:15] This complexity means that even a seemingly simple tariff can trigger a global chain reaction, creating more consequences than anyone can plan for.
