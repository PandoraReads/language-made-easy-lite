# Why magnets stumped scientists for so long - Rachel Yang

![video_link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jG7ULeWUfDw)

## 字幕

[00:07-00:14] In the early 1900s, physicists Niels Bohr and Hendrika Johanna van Leeuwen

[00:14-00:17] separately discovered something shocking.

[00:17-00:25] According to every known rule of physics, magnetism was impossible.

[00:25-00:27] This finding made no sense.

[00:27-00:38] Humans had relied on magnetic compasses for a thousand years, and anyone who had ever held two magnetized metals could tell you that some invisible force was acting on them.

[00:38-00:43] Bohr and van Leeuwen knew they were missing something— but what?

[00:43-00:49] By the 1920s, physicists had learned quite a bit about the fundamentals of matter.

[00:49-00:57] They knew that every material is made of atoms, each comprised of a nucleus containing protons and orbited by electrons.

[00:57-01:09] They also knew that both of these particles have a few fundamental properties, including a small amount of mass, an electrical charge, and some amount of magnetism.

[01:09-01:13] The origin of these intrinsic properties remains an ongoing mystery.

[01:13-01:22] But the result is that every electron and proton is a tiny magnet that works just like their full-size counterparts.

[01:22-01:29] Both have invisible magnetic fields that radiate out in closed loops from one end to the other.

[01:29-01:39] These magnetic poles are attracted to the dense field lines of the opposite pole of other magnets, and repelled by fields flowing in the opposite direction.

[01:39-01:42] Of course, researchers still had questions.

[01:42-01:45] What determines the direction of these magnetic fields?

[01:45-01:52] And if every atom is composed of tiny magnets, why isn't everything magnetic?

[01:52-02:03] The answers emerged with the birth of quantum mechanics: a new branch of physics exploring how the laws of nature work differently at the sub-atomic level.

[02:03-02:18] In 1926, a pair of Dutch physicists discovered that— in addition to mass, charge, and magnetism— particles had another fundamental property: quantum spin.

[02:18-02:29] This is a confusing label because while nothing is actually spinning, spin does refer to a kind of innate direction that influences a particle’s behavior.

[02:29-02:31] And in the 1930s, Nobel Prize laureate John H.

[02:31-02:42] Van Vleck discovered that this property held the answer to countless questions about magnetism— including Bohr and van Leeuwen’s.

[02:42-02:49] Basically, the direction of a particle’s spin determines the direction of its magnetic field.

[02:49-02:59] And since electrons have much stronger magnetic fields than protons, the direction of their spin determines whether or not something is magnetic.

[02:59-03:13] If enough of a material's electrons spin in the same direction, their many tiny magnetic fields will align, turning the material into a magnet with a field pointing in that direction.

[03:13-03:17] The more aligned electrons, the stronger the magnet.

[03:17-03:29] On the other hand, if a material is full of electrons with competing spins their fields cancel each other out, leaving you with something non-magnetic.

[03:29-03:38] Most elements have electrons that come in pairs and spin in opposite directions, which is why most materials aren’t magnetic.

[03:38-03:45] But iron, nickel, cobalt, and a few rare metals prefer to leave some of their electrons unpaired.

[03:45-03:53] This doesn’t automatically make them magnetic— just because their electrons are unpaired doesn’t mean they always spin in the same direction.

[03:53-03:59] However, they can be nudged into alignment by interacting with neighboring atoms.

[03:59-04:11] And researchers can kick-start this process by applying a strong magnetic field or by heating and cooling the material inside a weaker magnetic field.

[04:11-04:17] These metals will stay magnets permanently unless their electrons are scrambled

[04:17-04:21] by intense heat or an extremely strong magnetic field.

[04:21-04:26] But this magnetization process can make anything magnetic temporarily.

[04:26-04:32] Felix Bloch and Edward Mills Purcell took advantage of this in 1945

[04:32-04:37] when they invented nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy.

[04:37-04:48] This technology uses a strong magnetic field to temporarily align a material's protons, then a weaker field to knock specific molecules out of alignment.

[04:48-05:01] The machine then tracks the electromagnetic energy expended by the jostled protons as they realign, allowing researchers to map the material's molecular structure.

[05:01-05:12] This invention laid the groundwork for MRI machines, which use a similar approach to magnetize the water in our bodies and map our tissues.

[05:12-05:20] Of course, NMR and MRI are just two of countless modern technologies that rely on magnetism.

[05:20-05:29] Computer hard drives, electric motors, speakers, wireless communication— all these inventions prove that not only does magnetism exist, but studying this phenomenon remains endlessly attractive.
