# Everything you need to know about bird flu - Benjamin Anderson

## 字幕

[00:06-00:13] In March 2024, US veterinarians were facing down a mystery.

[00:13-00:23] On dairy farms in Texas and nearby states, cows were losing their appetite and producing thick, discolored milk.

[00:23-00:31] At the same time, cats on these farms were being diagnosed with an unknown neurological disease.

[00:31-00:49] For weeks, all their tests for common cattle diseases came back negative until lab results finally uncovered the culprit: influenza A subtype H5N1 — more commonly known as bird flu.

[00:49-00:56] This wasn’t the first time a virus adapted for birds infected non-avian animals.

[00:56-01:05] In fact, every human influenza pandemic on record has been linked to some strain of bird flu.

[01:05-01:12] So what is it about these viruses that makes them so frighteningly flexible?

[01:12-01:18] Of the four groups of flu viruses, all bird flus fall into group A.

[01:18-01:26] Within this group, the viruses are organized into subtypes defined by two viral proteins.

[01:26-01:34] Hemagglutinin binds to host cell receptors, allowing the virus to enter the cell and replicate.

[01:34-01:39] And neuraminidase helps those new viruses leave the cell.

[01:39-01:52] Currently, we’ve discovered 18 types of hemagglutinin and 11 types of neuraminidase, and different subtypes of influenza A use different combinations.

[01:52-01:58] For example, the combo of hemagglutinin 5 and neuraminidase 1

[01:58-02:01] is called H5N1.

[02:01-02:08] These proteins are necessary for influenza A to enter and exit host cells,

[02:08-02:13] so they generally determine which animals a strain can infect.

[02:13-02:26] But birds, especially wild aquatic birds, have receptors for most known versions of these proteins, making them susceptible to nearly all combinations.

[02:26-02:38] Worse still, these waterfowl shed the virus through their feces into lakes and ponds, increasing the odds of infection for any bird using those bodies of water.

[02:38-02:44] And since the viruses don’t usually make these birds actively sick,

[02:44-02:50] they can act as reservoirs for disease— flying around and spreading their strain.

[02:50-02:55] Thankfully, humans are mostly safe from all these flus.

[02:55-03:03] We only have receptors for a few viral proteins, protecting us from most combinations.

[03:03-03:12] And while bird flus can sometimes bind to receptors in our airways, our more vulnerable receptors are deep in our lungs.

[03:12-03:18] This makes infection less likely and transmission less efficient

[03:18-03:23] compared to common human flus that target receptors in our nose and throat.

[03:23-03:28] Unfortunately, influenza has other tricks up its sleeves.

[03:28-03:38] Viruses have antigens— specific molecules that our immune system can recognize and attack to fight against an infection.

[03:38-03:45] But two processes are constantly changing these molecules and challenging our immune systems.

[03:45-03:53] The first is antigenic drift, where antigens change slowly over time due to random mutations.

[03:53-04:02] This makes it harder for immune systems to recognize the virus, but the process is relatively slow and trackable.

[04:02-04:09] In fact, we manage the antigenic drift of human flu by updating our flu shots every year.

[04:09-04:16] The much more sudden and potentially dangerous process is antigenic shift.

[04:16-04:22] This happens when two or more different influenza viruses meet inside a host

[04:22-04:37] and swap entire gene segments, instantly creating a new virus with all-new abilities— including the potential to bind to different receptors and infect different species.

[04:37-04:41] This is why bird flus are so concerning.

[04:41-04:50] Since birds are vulnerable to so many influenza A subtypes, they’re natural reservoirs for antigenic shifts.

[04:50-04:53] Consider our dairy farm mystery.

[04:53-04:59] While H5N1 used to infect mostly birds, in the past few years,

[04:59-05:09] it’s been found in over 70 mammal species, including cats, dairy cows, foxes, seals, and bears.

[05:09-05:16] Each new mammalian infection gives the virus more chances to drift or shift,

[05:16-05:22] potentially spilling over into humans, and igniting a pandemic.

[05:22-05:31] And since our immune systems aren’t trained to recognize avian influenza proteins, the effects of these viruses can be deadly.

[05:31-05:33] So, should we panic?

[05:33-05:35] Not yet.

[05:35-05:45] Despite the fact that influenza is ubiquitous in birds across the globe, bird flu infections in humans are still relatively rare.

[05:45-05:49] What's most important is to stay vigilant.

[05:49-05:59] Scientists are working around the clock to monitor ongoing viral evolution, test vaccines, and strengthen biosecurity.

[05:59-06:02] Everything could change with a single outbreak, ao the only way to win this viral arms race is to stay one flap ahead.
